A week ago General Kornilov's trains were coming up the line to hang the Soviet, and the whole city
knew it. They never arrived. Railwaymen shunted them into sidings, agitators talked his Cossacks out
of the fight, and the factories took the rifles the government handed out in its panic. They have
not given them back.

Now Petrograd is full of armed workers, angry soldiers and rumors. The bread queues on the Vyborg
side start before dawn. Kerensky still sits in the Winter Palace, but nobody you know expects him to
last the winter. This afternoon the word on every tram is that Trotsky is out of Kresty.

You're halfway across the Liteyny Bridge when a lorry full of Red Guards grinds to a stop beside
you and blocks the traffic. A woman in a leather jacket leans down from the back, one hand on a
rifle sling.

"You. Going to the Smolny or not? The Soviet sits tonight and we've room for one more."

Behind the lorry a tram driver is leaning on his bell. Nobody moves.
